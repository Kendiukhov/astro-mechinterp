Astronomy foundation models are trained on two things at once: survey pixels, and catalogue products a pipeline extracted from those pixels. Those catalogues are incomplete at a measurable rate, and we show that a model trained on both learns to trust the catalogue over the pixels -- turning a routine pipeline failure into a systematic error in the science. We audit AION-1, a transformer trained on over 200 million objects.

Altering only the segmentation map, with every image pixel byte-identical, changes every quantity the model reports -- flux, size, ellipticity, redshift -- by 110-4400 times a matched placebo. The model is not doing aperture photometry on the region the mask encloses; it asks whether the mask marks a source at the field centre. Its response tracks central mask coverage (r = 0.47) more than the light enclosed (r = 0.30), and across 322 blended systems it ignores how the pipeline divided the light (R = -0.006). Given wrong catalogue photometry, it does nine times worse than with none.

The pipeline leaves 3.68% of targets with no segment covering their position, over 5000 cutouts. Representing a miss by the fields the pipeline actually returns, and propagating that rate through the model's photometric redshifts, shifts tomographic mean redshifts by a median 0.71 times the LSST DESC requirement over 40 assignments and exceeds it in 12. Drawing misses by their measured magnitude dependence rather than uniformly does not change this. Positional errors take the worst bin to 8.3 times. Spectra remove the effect; withholding the detection channel removes it at no cost.

Two limits are in the tokeniser: the image codec resolves 28 effective states on source patches against 934 for spectra, and the redshift readout is limited by its own grid. Sparse dictionaries are unreliable handles: across 15, recovery spans 26-75%, moving 18 points on the seed.
