Dear Editors,

I submit "A survey detection channel overrides the pixels in an astronomical foundation model,
and biases tomographic mean redshifts" for consideration as a Research Article in PLOS ONE.

Astronomical foundation models are trained on survey images together with the catalogue
products that a software pipeline has already extracted from those same images. Those
catalogues fail at a measurable rate. This paper shows experimentally that a model trained on
both learns to rely on the catalogue rather than the pixels, and measures what that costs a
downstream scientific measurement.

The work is primary research, not a commentary or a review. Its spine is a series of controlled
interventions on a released model's inputs, each reported against a matched placebo and with
bootstrap intervals. Holding every image pixel byte-identical and editing only the survey
segmentation map moves every quantity the model reports by 110 to 4400 times a matched placebo
edit. Further interventions localise the mechanism: the model responds to whether the mask
marks a source at the field centre, not to the light the mask encloses, and across 322 blended
systems it is entirely insensitive to how the pipeline divided light between overlapping
objects. We then measure the pipeline's own failure rate directly over 5000 cutouts (3.68% of
targets have no segment covering their position) and propagate it, obtaining a shift in
tomographic mean redshift of a median 0.71 times the LSST Dark Energy Science Collaboration
requirement across 40 realisations. The paper ends with a mitigation that costs nothing
measurable.

I believe this fits PLOS ONE's criteria. The journal evaluates research on scientific validity
and methodology rather than perceived significance, and states that interdisciplinary work and
negative results are in scope. Both matter here. The paper sits between machine learning and
observational astronomy, and two of its results are negative: deblending errors do not
propagate through the channel we study, and sparse dictionaries, the standard interpretability
tool, prove unreliable as causal handles across a 15-dictionary sweep. Neither result would
survive a significance filter, and both are worth recording.

Three disclosures.

First, the study audits AION-1, a model released by the Polymathic AI collaboration. It does
not contradict any claim those authors make: it measures a behaviour their paper does not
characterise, and it was only possible because they released weights, tokenisers and code
openly. I have no affiliation with, funding from, or other connection to that group. I would
nonetheless ask that referees be selected from outside that collaboration.

Second, a preprint is posted as arXiv:2608.23626 under a CC BY 4.0 licence. I note the licence
explicitly for the record; it is compatible with PLOS ONE's own CC BY 4.0 publishing licence.

Third, a large language model was used substantially in this work: to write the analysis code,
run the experiments, produce the figures, and draft the text. This is disclosed in the Methods
section in the form PLOS requires, naming the tool, describing which parts of the study it
affected, and setting out how its outputs were validated. The validation is not nominal. Every
numeric claim in the article is checked programmatically against the recorded output that
produced it; every figure is regenerated from that recorded output with no model call; the
bibliography was machine-checked so that all 85 entries resolve to a live identifier; and two
conclusions I had reached did not survive re-derivation and were withdrawn before submission.

All code and per-experiment results are public. The result files underlying every figure, table
and numeric claim accompany this submission as S1 File.

The manuscript is not under consideration elsewhere, and all data are from public archives.

Finally, for completeness: I have published a closely related study applying the same family
of methods -- data probes, matched controls, causal interventions and sparse autoencoders
tested against an external ground truth -- to a plant genomic foundation model (Computational
Biology and Chemistry 126:109438, doi:10.1016/j.compbiolchem.2026.109438). That paper shares
methodology with this one but no data, no model and no results; it is cited here as prior work.
I mention it so the overlap is on the record rather than discovered.
